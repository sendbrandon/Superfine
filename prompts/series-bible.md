# [SERIES NAME] — Series Bible

A show built from the DNA of the DoorDash argument clip. Not the clip on
repeat. The clip's directing becomes the house style. The clip's two people
become two of four characters. The clip itself becomes the cold open of the
pilot. Everything else is new.

The unit of production is the **scene**: one 15-second generation. An episode
is five to eight scenes cut together.

**The prompt you paste is one paragraph, in the voice of the original.** Spoken,
hyped, profane, "right?". The original prompt's voice is part of why the clip
came out the way it did, so the voice stays. What gets enhanced is the
information inside the paragraph: who is in the room and what they look like,
the one light source, the shape of the cut, one or two exact lines. Sections 2
through 6 below are the thinking. Section 7 is what you paste.

Character handles (DOT, FLEECE, CANVAS, DAGGER) are working handles taken from
the wardrobe. Replace with real names everywhere once chosen. Roles are
proposals; overwrite them.

---

## 1. The show

**Premise.** Four broke roommates in a falling-apart apartment. Not broke and
sad. Broke and litigious. Every episode is one petty principle pushed all the
way to the end, with survival stakes underneath it. The apartment is home
base. The show leaves it constantly: the fire escape, the bodega, the
laundromat, the landlord, the job, the bus, the ER, the party.

**Engine.** Someone violates a rule of living together. The crew prosecutes.
The trial spreads through the day and out into the city. The verdict lands on
the wrong person.

**Season spine.** The power gets cut in the pilot. The season is the four of
them trying to get it back on without paying for it, one scheme per episode,
while the standalone trials keep happening in the dark. Finale: the power
comes back. The apartment in full light for the first time. It is disgusting.
They turn it off.

**Format.** 9:16 vertical. Episodes of 75 to 120 seconds, built from 15-second
scenes. Cold open is the crime. Last scene is the verdict. Every scene ends on
a face, which is also the cut point.

## 2. The tone tail (the back half of every paragraph)

This is the original prompt's back half with a few words added. Paste it at
the end of every scene paragraph, filling the two brackets.

> It's a high-energy, high-paced, quick-cut 15-second scene, five to seven cuts, everybody playing straight into the lens like the camera is the other person, one wide shot [of what], and it ends on [whose] face. It competes with fucking Rick and Morty meets fucking Larry David meets Patrice O'Neal, vulgar, cutting each other up on purpose, every insult about who the other person actually is, not overdone, done with taste, right? This is raw, vulgar comedy that has that action-packed, hyper-cut cinematic quality, photoreal, handheld, grainy, one light source, no music, dead fucking serious, nobody winks.

What the added words buy you: "straight into the lens" locks the clip's
signature shot. "One wide" resets the room. "Ends on [whose] face" gives you
the cut point to the next scene. "One light source" is the show's look.
"Photoreal, handheld, grainy, no music" keeps the model from drifting toward
polish or a score. "Nobody winks" keeps it dead serious.

## 5. Anatomy of a scene paragraph

Same order as the original prompt. Situation first, format second, tone last.

1. **Who and what.** Who is in the room by `@Image` tag and what they are doing. Open on the situation the way the original opened on "these two people are arguing."
2. **Where and the light.** The location and the one light source, said plainly: "the only light is."
3. **What happens.** The beat, with one or two exact lines in quotes. The model performs the rest.
4. **The shape of the cut.** From the tone tail: quick-cut, five to seven cuts, faces straight into the lens, one wide (say what is in it), ends on whose face.
5. **The tone.** The rest of the tone tail, verbatim.

Two to three hundred words. If it runs longer, cut the description, not the
voice.

---

## 6. Scene rules (the filter)

1. **Every scene is one petty principle with survival stakes underneath.** If the concept arrives without stakes, add the bill.
2. **Nobody argues about the thing.** They argue the principle behind the thing, and the principle becomes a worldview inside four seconds. (Rick and Morty.)
3. **Every insult is a diagnosis.** About the other person's habits, history, and body of work in this apartment. If it could be said to a stranger, cut it. (Patrice O'Neal.)
4. **Evidence in frame.** The crime is a physical object and the wide shows it.
5. **One light per scene.** It changes when the location changes. Never two sources.
6. **End on a face.** That is the cut point to the next scene.
7. **Dead serious.** Nobody knows it is funny. No smirks, no winks, no bits.
8. **Four in the room is still a two-hander.** The other two are witnesses. One of them gets the button.
9. **The verdict lands on the wrong person.** (Larry David.)

## Appendix B. Director-style VFX prompting

For scenes where an effect has to land. The tone tail stays. What changes is
that the effect is described as physics, not named.

1. **Name the EyeCandy technique, then say what it does in one sentence of physics.** The name is for us. The sentence is what the model renders. "Split diopter" means nothing to it. "Her in the foreground left, sharp, him across the aisle in the middle, sharp" does.
2. **Numbers, not adjectives.** Distance in meters, time in seconds, size in centimeters, speed in meters per second. "Fingers stretch thirty centimeters in two seconds," not "fingers grow."
3. **Orientation before motion.** Where everyone is relative to the camera and each other before anything moves. Left bench, right bench, two and a half meters, dead center.
4. **Say what does not move.** "He does not stand up. Only the tentacles move." A model fills silence with motion.
5. **Five questions per effect.** What happens to the subject, the environment, the background, the camera, and the physics. One clause each. If a question has no answer, skip it, don't pad it.
6. **No text anywhere in the environment.** Blank ad panels, blank signs.
7. **Length.** Longer than a tone-only prompt, shorter than a shot list. Every sentence carries a number or an orientation or it goes.
8. **Before-state, action, after-state, then chain.** Every effect and every reaction gets its starting state and its ending state written down, even a face. "Before the zoom he's slack, thirty percent of the frame, mouth closed; it lands in three frames and he's eighty percent of the frame, eyes wide, jaw open." The next beat begins from that after-state ("From that face he turns to her"), so the model carries the scene forward instead of resetting. Same for the room: "Still room, everyone seated" becomes "everyone standing, paper in the air," and the next beat inherits the paper in the air.
9. **One big dynamic element per scene, with taste.** Something cinematic happens to the room (a car through a screen, a frozen riot) inside a place the audience can name in one second. Austin Powers, Men in Black: heightened, never off the wall. The crew does not move for it.

### Two lengths

**Vibe form, the default.** The length of the original car-chase prompt:
about 150 words of direction plus the tone tail. It describes energy, not a
set. The setting is an adjective or two ("dimly lit, broken-down
apartment"), never an inventory: no fixtures, no light sources, no furniture,
no blocking, no numbers, no lines. The action is described as beats of
energy the way you'd pitch it out loud (cops hanging out the window, reaction
shots on the sidewalk, POV down a scope). The camera is energy words: quick-cut,
dynamic camera following, faces straight into the lens, slow motion on the
big moments. Then the subject of the argument and the ending image. The model
builds the room, the light, and the lines, and it builds them well.

Carry forward only what a run proved has to be said, in a few words each:
slow motion instead of a freeze, "without looking up" for a flat character,
"faces straight into the lens."

**Long form, the repair.** The before-and-after chain from rules 8 and 9,
used only after a vibe-form run drifts on a specific beat. Fix that beat,
not the whole prompt.

Never script lines unless the line is the joke.

EyeCandy techniques confirmed on the site and used so far: Split Diopter, Morphing, Transformation, Crash Zoom, Speed Ramp, Whip Pan, Dolly Zoom, Snorricam, Dutch Angle, Pass Through, Crash Cut.
